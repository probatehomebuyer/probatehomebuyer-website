Inherited a House That Needs Work? Your Sale Options

A practical guide for executors and families dealing with an inherited house that needs repairs, clearance, modernisation or a faster as-is sale.

Can you sell an inherited house that needs work?

Yes. An inherited house does not usually need to be repaired or modernised before it can be sold. The practical decision is whether spending estate money and time is likely to improve the estate's net result, or whether an as-is sale offers a better balance of price, certainty and work.

Before choosing, record the condition, understand the estate's authority to act and compare like-for-like figures. A higher headline price after renovation is not automatically a better outcome once repair bills, holding costs, fees and delay are included.

A house needing work can still be saleable. The executor's task is to document the condition, compare realistic routes and keep evidence for the decision.

First steps for an executor

  1. Confirm who is authorised to deal with the estate and ask the estate solicitor when contracts and completion can take place.
  2. Tell the home insurer that the owner has died and check the policy's occupancy, inspection, heating and security conditions.
  3. Secure doors and windows, deal with urgent leaks or hazards and keep a dated record of visits.
  4. Photograph each room and visible defect before clearance or building work starts.
  5. Obtain condition-based valuations and written quotes before committing estate funds.
  6. Keep beneficiaries informed and record why the chosen sale route appears reasonable for the estate.

Understand the condition before deciding

Common issues include dated kitchens and bathrooms, damp, leaks, old wiring, roof repairs, overgrown gardens and contents left inside. Some defects affect presentation; others can restrict mortgage lending or create an urgent safety or insurance issue.

Executors do not need to diagnose everything themselves. Photographs, contractor quotes, survey comments and comparable sales can help distinguish essential protection work from optional refurbishment. Keep that evidence with the estate records.

Four ways to sell a house that needs work

1. Repair, clear and use an estate agent

This may suit an estate with available funds, enough time and agreement between executors and beneficiaries. A well-presented home may attract more owner-occupiers, but the estate carries the cost and risk of the work before a sale is certain.

2. List it as-is with an estate agent

An agent can market the current condition to renovators and other open-market buyers. Ask how the asking price was supported, who the likely buyer is and whether mortgage valuation or survey findings could affect the transaction.

3. Sell at auction

Auction can expose a property needing substantial work to buyers who expect renovation. Ask about the reserve, entry and legal-pack fees, the timetable and what happens if the reserve is not met. The result is not guaranteed until a bid is accepted and the auction terms become binding.

4. Compare a direct as-is offer

A direct buyer assesses the property in its present condition, so repairs, contents and risk can be reflected in the offer. This can reduce preparation and chain risk, although the offer will normally be below the price a fully renovated property might achieve. Will repairs add more value than they cost?

Start with evidence rather than a general percentage. Ask for written quotes, realistic before-and-after valuations and an estimated selling timetable. Then compare the likely net proceeds for each route.

  • Include building work, clearance, certificates and contingency.
  • Allow for estate-agent, auction, legal and finance costs where applicable.
  • Add continuing council tax, insurance, utilities, security and maintenance.
  • Consider who will supervise contractors and what happens if hidden defects are found.
  • Compare certainty and timing as well as the estimated final amount.

Minor cleaning, gardening or safety work may help without becoming a full refurbishment. Executors should take appropriate valuation, legal or tax advice where the amounts or duties are uncertain.

Valuations, quotes and probate timing

Should executors get a valuation first?

Executors usually need to understand the estate's value for probate and inheritance-tax reporting. The valuation should reflect the property's condition at the relevant date. Keep photographs, defect notes, comparable evidence and repair estimates that help explain the figure.

Can you obtain quotes or offers before probate is granted?

Families can generally gather information, request valuations and speak to contractors or buyers while probate is in progress. Whether an executor can instruct work, exchange contracts or complete depends on the estate and their legal authority, so confirm the position with the estate solicitor before committing the estate.

Offers can sometimes be discussed subject to contract and probate. Make sure every party understands that wording and any conditions attached to the proposed timetable.

Do you need to clear the house before selling?

Not always. Open-market buyers may prefer a cleared house, while some auction or direct buyers will consider furniture and unwanted contents in place. First identify personal papers, valuables, items specifically left by the will and anything beneficiaries wish to keep.

Questions to ask before accepting a direct offer

  • Is the buyer purchasing directly, and can they provide evidence of funds?
  • Are legal, survey, clearance or other deductions expected?
  • Is the price fixed after inspection, and in what circumstances could it change?
  • Is the offer subject to probate, contract or any other condition?
  • Who chooses the solicitor and pays each side's legal costs?
  • Can the completion date move if the grant or estate administration takes longer?
  • What evidence can the executor keep to support the decision?

Compare the written terms, not only the headline number. A transparent offer should identify assumptions and give the executor enough time to take advice. Our broader guide to selling an inherited house explains the direct-sale process and alternatives.

When an as-is direct sale may make sense

  • The house needs refurbishment before many mortgage-backed buyers will be interested.
  • The estate cannot or does not wish to fund and supervise works upfront.
  • The property is empty and accumulating holding costs or security risk.
  • Beneficiaries value a defined timetable and fewer moving parts.
  • Clearance, access or condition makes repeated viewings difficult.

It is not automatically the best route. Some estates will reasonably choose an estate agent or auction after comparing net proceeds, risks and timescales.
